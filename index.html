01

The claim

On May 5, 2026, Robert Breaker went live with Robert Potthoff for “Faith in the Blood is NOT a Modern Doctrine.” He read quotes from church history and said they prove his salvation theology has historical backing.

I checked every one of them. None of them can be shown to hold his theology.

What I mean by “modern”

When I say his salvation theology is modern, I mean this specific teaching: you aren’t saved unless you single out the blood of Jesus Christ as the distinct object of your saving faith, and trust “with all your heart” in that blood, the physical shed blood, for salvation.

He may say “believe the gospel,” but he redefines every part of the death, burial and resurrection to be about the blood:

Asking God to save you is a “work”

The other half of his theology: if you call on God as a condition for salvation, he says that’s faith plus works.

He isn’t even consistent about it. At points he says the confession of Romans 10:10 is a byproduct of faith, which is John Calvin’s view. At other points he says the calling of Romans 10:13 isn’t a mouth response at all, but an automatic heart response, using 2 Timothy 2:22, even though Christ says what comes out of the heart comes out of the mouth (Matthew 12:34).

The best example is me

Gene Kim told me that Jesus died, was buried and rose again, so His blood could wash away my sins. To receive that, I asked God to save me:

According to Robert Breaker, I’m not saved and I’m on my way to hell, even though I trusted the blood, because I asked. Any reading of Romans 10:9-15 that makes calling a condition for salvation is, to him, a damnable gospel.

The three tests

So for someone in history to hold his theology, they have to pass all three:

1Salvation is by faith alone.

2The blood of Jesus Christ is the distinct object of saving faith, and you must specifically, consciously focus on it to be saved.

3Calling on God as a condition for salvation (Romans 10:9-16) is a work.

Nobody in church history passes all three. So instead, Breaker finds anyone who mentions the blood and says they fit. Every person below gets tried on all three.

03

The early church

Ignatius of Antioch c. 110

The quote is real. But Ignatius says their faith is as immovable as Christ’s cross. That’s it. Nothing about faith alone, nothing singling out the blood, nothing about calling being a work. Reading “faith in the blood” into it is eisegesis: putting your own presuppositions into the text.

A cross is mentioned. How does that make the blood the distinct object of faith in Ignatius’s soteriology? And what about 1 Corinthians 15:3-4, or Romans 10, where saving faith is believing the resurrection, confessing Jesus as Lord, and calling on His name?

There’s a backstory here. In Chapter 8 of his book, Breaker originally quoted Smyrnæans 6:1: “if they believe not in the blood of Christ, shall, in consequence, incur condemnation.” Tony pointed out that “the VERY next paragraph (Chapter 7) lets you know Ignatius is speaking of the EUCHARIST.” So Breaker dropped 6:1 and moved to 1:3, which is why he has to strain so hard to make it agree with him.

Ignatius was writing against Docetists, who said Jesus’ body wasn’t really physical. Even if he meant the blood and not the Eucharist, the blood stands in for the whole death, burial and resurrection, because that is exactly what the Docetists denied.

Verdict Fails all three.

Clement of Rome c. 96

Breaker read two quotes from 1 Clement, close to J. B. Lightfoot’s translation. Read in context, both of them work against him.

The first is about Rahab and the scarlet thread:

Here’s how Breaker explained it:

Breaker, that isn’t what it says. Clement doesn’t say “believe in the blood and hope on God.” He says “believe and hope on God.” Both verbs have the same object, and it’s God. You had to add “in the blood” yourself.

The blood is in the sentence, but as the means of redemption: “through the blood of the Lord.” It’s what redemption comes through, not what you believe in. Roberts-Donaldson reads the same way: redemption “should flow through the blood of the Lord to all those who believe and hope in God.” Nowhere does Clement tell anyone to single out the blood and trust it. Test 2 fails.

The second quote:

The blood was “shed for our salvation,” and what it won is “the grace of repentance.” Clement doesn’t tell anyone to trust the blood. He tells them what the blood made possible.

And “fix our eyes” doesn’t make the blood the object of faith either. Clement gives the same command about things nobody trusts for salvation: “Let us fix our eyes on them that ministered perfectly unto His excellent glory” (9:2), and “let us look steadfastly unto the Father and Maker of the whole world” (19:2). It means look at something until you understand it, and 7:4 tells you what to understand: “how precious it is unto His Father.”

Then Clement shows what that “grace of repentance” looks like, in the very next lines:

“By their supplications” is one Greek word, hiketeusantes (ἱκετεύσαντες), the word for a suppliant begging for mercy. The Ninevites didn’t single out the blood. They were Gentiles, “aliens from God,” who repented and begged God for mercy, and Clement says they “received salvation,” the same salvation the blood was “shed for” three verses earlier.

Breaker, you call asking God to save you the “Beggars gospel.” Clement holds up begging God as the model of how salvation is received, right after the quote you read. Test 3 fails.

Clement tells his own readers to come the same way:

Those are written to a church, so 7:7 is still the case of lost sinners getting saved. But in Clement, begging God for mercy and confessing to Him are never treated as works. They’re the one thing he says God asks for.

To be fair to Breaker, Clement does pass test 1. He teaches that we are not justified by works:

Chapter 30 doesn’t contradict that. “Being justified by works and not by words” (30:3) is said to people who are already “the special portion of a Holy God” (30:1), and the contrast is deeds against talk: “Doth the ready talker think to be righteous?” (30:4). It’s about how people who already belong to God show they’re righteous, not how a sinner is first justified.

But faith alone is only one of Breaker’s three points. Clement never makes the blood the object of faith, and he holds up begging God for mercy as how the Ninevites received salvation. Breaker, you gave me these quotes.

Verdict Fails tests 2 and 3.

08

Conclusion

Robert Breaker quotes anyone who mentions the blood of Jesus Christ, then says they teach his distinct theology: that the blood itself is the object of faith, and you must single it out to be saved.

He leans on people defending the penal substitutionary atonement, people opposing John MacArthur’s view that the blood just means the death, and people who believe the blood is on the mercy seat in heaven. None of that is his theology. Anyone who says “the blood” is, to him, “a Breakerite.” I plead the blood daily, as Dr. Ruckman did. He could take me out of context the same way.

His salvation theology has no historical backing. It is a 20th-century invention. Whether you pick T. T. Martin or Robert Ray Breaker II, his father, it doesn’t change much which 20th-century man came up with it. Not Robert Breaker at his best, and not AI at its best, can prove otherwise.

The worst part is that he seems to have no regret: keeping the Kindle up after he apologized, and quoting anyone who made a passing comment about the blood as support. His methods fail every historical standard.

The Holy Spirit leads every Christian into ALL TRUTH. Robert Breaker has not been led.
